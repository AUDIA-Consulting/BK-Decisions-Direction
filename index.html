02 / The Reframe

From Acquisition Strategy to Role Architecture

The biggest shift since May 8 is not in any one decision. It is in how the question itself is framed. The May 8 document treated this as an acquisition strategy problem with seven sub-decisions. What the May 12, 14, 22, 24, and 27 conversations made clear is that the acquisition strategy is downstream of a more fundamental question. What role do Jason and Kenneth play in the business going forward, and what team shape does that role require? Get the role architecture right and the acquisition strategy falls out of it. Get the acquisition strategy right without the role architecture and the firm will be back at this same conversation in eighteen months.

Kenneth's articulation of the new posture on May 14, with Jason agreeing on the call, is the clearest expression of it. He described loving the hunt and wanting to be free of the kill. He described investing as a limited partner in a recent deal and the relief of not carrying the problems. He described wanting Ben and one or two other younger people to be the ones reaping most of the economic benefit on the active side, while he and Jason throttle back to a smaller share of the promote, do not guarantee loans anymore, and shift their personal capital into the LP seat. Eight days later, in his solo session with Claude on May 24, he put numbers on it. Roughly $3M each per year (~$6M total) deployed across deals where the firm participates as the capital and the relationships, not as the operator and the guarantor.

Kenneth deepened this rationale in his own words. With increasing frequency he wants to invest as a limited partner rather than always being the sponsor. Not stepping out of the sponsor role, but adding the LP posture alongside it. What draws him is being the investor without carrying the primary obligation, the relief of knowing someone else holds the pressure on the day-to-day problems while he still participates in the upside. He has carried that weight on every deal for years. A meaningful part of the reframe for him is simply getting out of the information loop: not hearing about every small problem, the ones he tends to sweat, and trusting Ben to handle the great majority while escalating only the ones that genuinely need a principal. His candid read is that he is often the one who pulls himself back into the loop, with a question or a quick email, and that the discipline to stay out of it is part of what makes the new posture work.

The shape that follows from the reframe. Jason and Kenneth as managing principals on an investment committee. Ben as the head of operations, with bench depth built beneath him to mitigate key-man risk and his investment voice preserved in every acquisition decision. A new senior hire as the head of investments, scoped on June 8 to own acquisitions, dispositions, financing, and equity relationships. The seven strategic decisions in the May 8 document are now sub-decisions inside this larger architecture. June 8 added a fourth pillar beneath all of it: clarity and empowerment, so the team can make the decisions it is capable of without routing every call up to the principals.

Decided · June 8 · Path D

Four Paths, Landed on Path D

PlazAmericas is a diamond-in-the-rough enclosed mall in an underserved Houston neighborhood. Four years of entitlement work are behind it. The catalytic lease with HEB is close, with roughly 90 percent of the open items now in HEB's court and a general-contractor strategy meeting set for the day after the session. The community impact case for finishing the redevelopment is real and worth honoring. The financial case is also real. The opportunity cost of Kenneth and Ben's direct operational involvement at the current intensity is what drove the decision. On June 8 the group resolved it: Path D, outsourced operations with ownership retained. The three paths below are preserved as the reasoning behind that choice.

Path A

Sell Outright

Take the value already created through entitlements and the catalytic HEB lease. Reclaim time and bandwidth immediately. Forfeit the community impact and the redevelopment upside. Sub-option surfaced May 27: sign and execute HEB first, then divest the remainder.

Path B

Owner-Level Operating Partner

Bring in an experienced enclosed-mall operator at the ownership level. Baker Katz retains meaningful economics and the community impact is preserved. Takes operational and redevelopment execution off the firm's plate. Originally the May 26 recommendation; held in reserve as the fallback if the Path D arrangement does not come together.

Path C

Finish as the "Final Big Project"

Baker Katz stays in the seat through the redevelopment as a one-time exception. Honors the work and the impact. Jason articulated the strongest emotional and community-impact case for this on May 27. Defers the role redefinition by two to four years. Not the path chosen, but the impulse behind it is preserved by holding ownership under Path D.

Path D · The Decision (June 8)

Outsourced Operations, Retain Ownership

A vendor like Boxer Properties takes security, facilities, housekeeping, maintenance, and day-to-day operations. Baker Katz retains ownership, leasing, and redevelopment control. Reversible if the arrangement does not work. Kenneth, Jason, and Ben all landed here on June 8. Boxer is the named candidate: it owns and operates LeBron Plaza, and the firm is already in contact with Andrew Siegel, the principal there. The draw is Boxer's experience owning and managing properties for the Hispanic community and running dozens of annual events. Target: buttoned up before the holidays, realistically a four-month effort rather than the sixty-to-ninety days first floated.

What Has to Be True Before Baker Katz Hands Over the Keys

Kenneth laid out the conditions that protect the firm if the arrangement is ever unwound. These are the diligence agenda for the Boxer conversation, starting with the Fort Worth site visit.

No worse off
Reversibility

If the engagement ends, the firm must not be left weaker than today. No employees more loyal to Boxer than to Baker Katz, and no dependence on Boxer's proprietary communication, accounting, or workflow systems that would leave the firm in a lurch.

A team, not B team
The Experienced Hands

Boxer's most experienced people sit in Fort Worth. The firm wants the benefit of those team members, which may mean one or more of them committing to be in Houston for a period. The whole reason to hire Boxer is that expertise, not a generic management contract.

Alignment
Security and Standards

Current security is outsourced to a private company. Moving to Boxer-employed guards could create a problem on termination. And some existing employees may resist the higher expectations Boxer will set, so the two firms need to align up front on how performance is handled.

What the Fort Worth Visit Showed · June 12

On June 11 Jason and Kenneth toured the Fort Worth mall with its owner for the first time, after three or four prior visits on their own. The walk-through reinforced the case. The common areas and the cleanliness reflected a real operator's attention to detail, and the owner has nearly doubled his headcount over a tenure of more than twenty years. Kenneth's read, silo by silo, is that this is the closest analog in the country to what Baker Katz wants to build, and that Boxer is at or near a "golden example" on the operating disciplines while Baker Katz keeps the strategic and retail judgment.

The caution: the owner is not a retail operator. He is discouraging a meaningful redevelopment investment and even the HEB anchor, on the view that a grocer would take the worst real estate just to join the project. Baker Katz believes that is plainly wrong, which is exactly why the engagement is scoped as outsourced operations rather than handing over the keys. The two firms have different objectives on the retail vision, and Baker Katz intends to keep that lane.

Maintenance and facilities, cost control, housekeeping, security, and accounting and collections. Kenneth rates several at or near a golden example, with cost control (vertically integrated and relentlessly cost-conscious) a value-add across every silo.

A lift, with help

Weekly, annual, and special events, sponsorships, and community outreach. Boxer is ahead, call it an eight to Baker Katz's six, but Baker Katz runs all of it today with one person and no support. Room to lift with Boxer's capacity plus Baker Katz's ideas.

Boxer covers office, small-shop, and routine leasing with heavy technology. Baker Katz retains the anchor leases (Ben's lane) and the proactive redevelopment tenant-mix strategy, where Boxer adds little. The open question is how to divide leasing and whose tools to use.

05 / SWOT

The Structural Picture

The SWOT below is structural. It describes the way the firm currently sources, screens, underwrites, and capitalizes deals, not the people executing those activities. The picture it paints is the one that informed the strategic decisions later in the document. Confirmed during the May 12 walkthrough and validated across the subsequent conversations.

Strengths

  • Twenty-plus years of experience and a proven model that has worked for Fund V investors
  • Decision-making agency. Equity is fully discretionary, which means the principals control deployment
  • Deep market knowledge and an existing relationship infrastructure in greater Houston
  • A low-fee posture that is genuinely attractive to LPs
  • An in-house tenant rep team that produces a constant flow of market data and tenant signal

Weaknesses

  • The current GP-friendly waterfall has worked well for Fund V's friends-and-family base. It is not market for institutional or scale-grade LPs and would likely need to evolve to support raising at the level the three-year goal requires
  • The "value add and opportunistic" mandate is not consistently operationalized when it requires committing on the firm's own conviction rather than tenant rep validation
  • The firm has historically passed on speculative deals based on a small sample of tenant feedback. The internal model for how that feedback gets weighed against the principals' own real estate conviction is not made explicit, which limits delegation
  • The weekly screening cadence is informal and conversational, not template-driven on the front end
  • Templates exist but are concentrated on the under-contract and PSA stage. Front-end screening templates are limited
  • Underwriting is gated on IRR even though the average hold runs five to ten years and IRR is largely an exit-driven metric
  • Investment criteria are written broadly enough that the firm's specific entrepreneurial posture is harder to communicate to brokers and prospective partners

Opportunities

  • Process redesign on the front end of deal screening, including a sell-hold analysis on every deal as part of the underwriting standard
  • A centralized CRM that combines relationships, deals, equity opportunities, and development pipeline in one place
  • A market-data rolodex that captures asks and signed rents, active deals, and tenant movement so the data the brokerage team is generating becomes accessible across the firm
  • AI tooling for the upfront work: lease abstracts, title document review, screening triage, and other administrative load that currently sits in human hours
  • Refining or rebuilding the internal Excel models so they align with the actual hold-and-operate profile of the existing portfolio
  • Targeted growth in the Top-20 list categories that are currently underdeveloped: lenders and capital, master-planned community developers, and land owners and brokers

Threats

  • Institutional capital flooding back into Texas retail. Fee-driven competitors charging two to five points on acquisition, plus annual asset management fees, plus disposition fees, against Baker Katz's promote-only economics
  • A post-COVID shift in the broker and owner ecosystem. Smaller shops, several of them with younger people in regular contact with the acquisitions team, are increasingly trying to deploy capital themselves rather than feeding deals to traditional buyers. Specific names showing up consistently: BCS, Palo Duro, DuWest, Wellspring, Jolink Wallace, and Ironbridge
  • The current capital structure makes raising at scale meaningfully harder. The friends-and-family base is loyal but limited in size, and the existing waterfall is unlikely to attract the kinds of investors who can fill the additional ~$55M of equity the three-year goal requires
